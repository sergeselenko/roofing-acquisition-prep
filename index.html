    The 12–36 month runway

    Prep done early is worth real money

    Practitioners converge on a 12–36 month prep runway for top pricing — and roofing has a clock other trades don't: safety records and revenue mix are judged on multi-year windows, so this year's fixes show up in a buyer's math two to three years from now. Here’s the order buyers wish you’d done it in. (Full checklist in Step 5.)

    36–24 months out

    Clean the foundation

    • Get QoE-ready: monthly closes, financials that tie to tax returns, documented add-backs
    • Start tagging every job retail / insurance / commercial in your books — buyers will bucket 24 months of jobs
    • Begin EMR improvement now — it's a 3-year rolling window, so credit takes 24–36 months

    24–12 months out

    De-risk the operation

    • Certify a non-owner licence qualifier — one advisor's guidance puts this 12–18 months pre-sale
    • Put sub crews on written agreements with their own insurance and COIs on file
    • Clean up the open-warranty and callback list before a buyer finds it
    • Grow around any customer over ~20% of revenue

    12–6 months out

    Grow what buyers pay for

    • Build the commercial maintenance book — contracted, assignable, documented as its own exhibit
    • Install a GM and prove the company runs while you're away
    • Turn on the AI levers in Step 3 — recovered jobs show up in trailing earnings

    Final 6 months

    Go to market ready

    • Don't launch on a storm-inflated trailing year — it invites the 3–5 year normalization re-cut
    • Go with contracted backlog on the books, not into your slow season
    • Pick the route (Step 4) and interview at least two advisors or buyers
    • Assemble the diligence vault (Step 5) before anyone asks

    Step one of every roofing deal — normalization

    Buyers will price the normalized number

    A hail year is not a run-rate. Buyers and their Quality of Earnings teams average revenue and earnings over 3–5 years (trailing-36 or trailing-60 months) to strip out storm spikes — a $4M-baseline roofer that books $9M after a hail event is not a $9M business, and deals priced as if it were get re-traded in diligence. So this page does the averaging first, in the open, and prices the normalized picture. Enter up to three years in Step 1's detail section and both pictures appear here.

    What your company could sell for — priced stream by stream

    Roofing isn't priced as one blob of revenue. Buyers split it: retail & project work at the base multiple, insurance/storm work at a discount to that multiple, and contracted commercial service at a premium — then add the streams up. Which earnings basis applies is ruled by size: below $1M of EBITDA, closed roofing deals price on SDE; at $1M+, on EBITDA. The basis used is always named below. Educational starting range, not a formal valuation.

    ↑ What supports the top of the range

    • High retail share — one real exit: a ~$12M-revenue roofer at ~75% retail sold to a PE platform for $8M; its founder notes most PE buyers look exclusively for retail (SPOTIO founder interview, 2026)
    • A contracted commercial maintenance book, diligenced as its own exhibit — account count, terms, renewal rate, assignability
    • Financials that survive a QoE: monthly closes, accrual books, job costing by job type
    • A GM running the week and a qualifier who isn't you
    • Contracted backlog with deposits — buyers want visibility, verbal pipeline counts for ~zero

    ↓ What pulls it down (or adds structure)

    • Storm share above ~40% of revenue — expect the spike to be averaged away and part of the price pushed into an earn-out
    • You are the licence qualifier, the top estimator, and the top salesperson in one person
    • Undocumented 1099 crews — a buyer's QoE can reclassify that labour cost straight out of your EBITDA
    • An open workmanship-warranty book with no list — surfaces as a holdback or escrow, not a multiple change
    • One customer or one sales producer carrying the revenue
    • EMR above 1.3, or an OSHA history you can't document
    Before you quote a price to anyone: get a market valuation from a roofing-experienced CPA or advisor and, as you approach $1M+ EBITDA, a sell-side Quality of Earnings review — above that line, buyers commission their own QoE on effectively every deal, and SBA lenders require one at a $3M+ purchase price. Also model after-tax proceeds: asset vs stock structure, seller-note risk, retainage and the working-capital peg can swing what you actually keep.

    What closed deals show — vs what articles claim

    Two literatures exist for roofing multiples, and they do not agree. This page's calculator runs only on the closed-deal evidence (left). The advertised numbers (right) are shown so you can recognise them when an article — or a buyer's opening pitch — quotes them. They are never blended.

    Closed-deal evidence — what the calculator uses

    286 closed roofing transactions: median sale price ≈ $540K at 2.2× SDE and 0.47× revenue (Legacy ETA, 2026 Roofing Business Sales Report — a buyer-side firm's own transaction dataset). Appraisal ranges for roofing sit in the same neighbourhood: SDE 1.88–2.73×, revenue 0.33–0.51× (Peak Business Valuation, 2025–26). Whole-company revenue multiples converge at ≈0.4–0.5× across three independent sources — a sanity check, never the price.

    A buyer's own published price list2.0–8.0× EBITDAInfinity Home Services — a PE-backed acquirer publishing what it says it pays, by revenue band. The source is itself a buyer.
    “Platform-quality” headlines6–12×Quoted in advisor marketing for large platforms. One disclosed platform sale, TopBuild–Progressive Roofing (2025), printed ≈9.1× on $438M of revenue.

    Closed data also shows the size curve is nearly flat: Axial's closed deals (Q3 2024–Q3 2026) put sub-$1M-EBITDA companies at a 3.9× median and $5M+ at 4.4×.     Step 3 of 6Grow the Value

    The AI value simulator

    Small leaks, priced like a buyer prices them

    Buyers don’t pay for revenue — they pay for profit, times a multiple. So every lever below runs the same honest chain: recovered work → extra revenue → extra profit at an incremental contribution margin → × your multiple → dollars of sale price. Two honesty rules are built in: vendor conversion claims are haircut 50% before they touch the model, and recovered install jobs are capped by the crew capacity you entered in Step 1 — your crews are the ceiling: demand you can't install this season isn't value yet. Only one lever re-rates your multiple: growing the contracted commercial service book, priced through Step 2's stream model. Everything else moves profit only.

    Your incremental contribution margin — applies to every lever

    Default 20%, adjustable from 10% to 30%. This is a modelling assumption, not a measured statistic — it's derived from published segment gross margins (Profitability Partners benchmarks: residential re-roof 28–42% gross, storm/insurance 35–50%, repairs & maintenance 40–65%, commercial 18–35%) less the variable selling, estimating, and supervision cost a recovered job still carries. Whole-business net margins for small roofers centre near 10% (range roughly 5–15%) across several compilations — if your recovered work is truly incremental, it should land above that; if it needs new crews, below.

    Incremental contribution margin20%
    One shared pool — no double counting: the missed-call, speed-to-lead, and review levers all chase the same lost customer, so their recovered jobs draw on one shared lost-demand pool — your monthly inquiries. The totals cap their combined recovery: the largest lever counts in full, the second at half, the third at a quarter, and the pool can never exceed the inquiries you actually lose. When that absolute cap binds, every lever in the pool is also scaled down proportionally to fit it — so final totals can sit below what the 100/50/25 split alone would give. Each lever card still shows its stand-alone figure; the headline totals apply the cap. Separately, recovered install jobs are capped at the spare crew capacity from your snapshot.

    How to read these numbers: they are modelled ranges from the published benchmarks and stated assumptions under each lever — not a promise. The shape is the point: one recovered roof a week is not “one roof” to a buyer — it's that roof's profit, every year, multiplied. Check your own missed-call count in your phone system before quoting any of this.

    Sources behind the levers

    • Missed calls (all vendor-reported platform data, definitions differ — never merged): 14% of home-services calls unanswered (CallRail, Jan 2025, narrow definition) · 27% unanswered (Invoca, May 2024) · 48% of callers never reach a human (Invoca, Jul 2026). Model default: 25% in normal hours, 40% after-hours (anchored to ServiceTitan's 41% weekend estimate) — labelled defaults with sliders, not benchmarks. Both rates run in the math: calls are split by the after-hours share set above the levers (a labelled assumption — no sourced call-volume split exists) and each rate applies to its share. Booking intent on answered calls ≈ 44% (Invoca: 45% of phone leads book on the call; ServiceTitan: ~42% average call booking rate). Fewer than 3% of callers sent to voicemail actually leave a message (Invoca, observed behaviour); absent answering coverage, assume ≤10% of missed callers are ever recovered.
    • Speed-to-lead: the famous “21× more likely to qualify in 5 minutes” figure is a ~2007–2011 B2B web-form study — it is never used as a multiplier here. The model uses a vendor-derived step (ServiceTitan 2025 benchmarks, second-hand: ~28% booking at a 42-minute response vs ~50–62% under 5 minutes), haircut to +15 percentage points.
    • Reviews: BrightLocal Local Consumer Review Survey 2026 (n=1,002) — 97% read reviews, 68% require ≥4.0 stars, 47% won't use a business with under 20 reviews, 74% only care about the last 3 months. Reviews are modelled as a threshold effect on lead flow; no source credibly converts stars into a percentage lift, so no per-star percentage is claimed.
    • Estimate follow-up: +6 percentage points of estimate-to-close (model default inside the +5–7pp case-derived range; one automated-follow-up case moved estimate-to-job 34% → 41% over 90 days). Baseline behaviour: 48% of salespeople never follow up at all (National Association of Sales Professionals, as cited 2026 — general sales, directional).
    • Supplements: +20% on the initial carrier scope, minus supplementing cost — the floor of a 100% vendor-reported field (three unrelated supplement vendors converge on ~20%; outsourced supplementing costs 10–15% of the recovery). Higher published claims (30–46%) are not used as defaults.
    • Aerial measurement: ≈1.5 hours saved per estimate (editorial estimate, range 1–2.5h, single source) plus one avoided site visit. Accuracy claims are vendor-reported (a Haag Engineering study commissioned by HOVER found 2.6% variance on n=40 homes). No close-rate lift from aerial tools is claimed anywhere on this page — any benefit runs through faster proposals, which is the speed lever. Measurement reports cost $13–$19 each (Roofr published pricing, vendor-reported).

    The buyer landscape

    Who is actually buying roofing companies

    Private-equity platforms in roofing grew from 17 at the start of 2023 to 56 by the end of 2024, acquiring 134 contractors in 2024 alone (single origin: Roofing Contractor's 2025 PE report, AXIA Advisors data; KPMG Corporate Finance independently describes 2025 volume as all-time highs). Verified active buyers include Tecta America (Altas Partners), Nations Roof (AEA Investors, 2024), Vertex Service Partners (Alpine Investors), Omnia Exterior Solutions (CCMP) — which bought Hero Roofing, the ~$12M-revenue, ~75%-retail company from Step 2 — and Ridgeline (Bertram Capital's Ignite strategy, publicly targeting $3–7.5M EBITDA). Two corrections worth knowing: Roofing Corp of America is no longer PE-owned (sold to FirstService Corp. in Dec 2023 — it now buys as a strategic), and Stronghouse Solutions' sponsor reportedly agreed in Sep 2026 to hand control to its creditors. These are market facts with sources, not partners of this page — nobody here can introduce you to them unless Step 6 says so. In closed deals, the honest size of the “PE premium” is modest: PE funds closed at a 5.1× EBITDA median vs 4.3× for strategics (Axial, 2026).

    If an SBA buyer is your likely buyer

    SBA 7(a) rules that shape smaller sales — verified & date-stamped

    Rules as of SOP 50 10 8.1, effective October 1, 2026 — verified October 30, 2026 against SBA's own publication and lender summaries of the new Appendix 15. SBA reissued 8.1 once already within two months of release, so confirm the current version with your lender before relying on any of this.

    10% equity injection — cannot be reducedFor an initial acquisition, the buyer must inject at least 10% of total project cost (price + fees + working capital), and unlike some other deal types it cannot be reduced or eliminated. On a $2M deal, that's $200K.
    Half the injection must be the buyer's ownAt least half must come from “unlimited” sources (the buyer's unborrowed cash and equivalents). All “limited” sources combined — standby seller notes, other standby debt, non-controlling minority investors — are capped at 50% of the injection. This aggregate cap is new in 8.1.
    Seller note counts only on full standbyA seller note counts toward the injection only if it's on full standby — no principal and no interest — for the entire term of the 7(a) loan, subordinated to the lender. A note that's being paid is allowed, but counts as debt in the coverage test, not equity. The old 24-month standby rules are dead.
    No phased buyoutsSBA does not finance multi-step buyouts. A partial sale runs as a single-step Owner Buyout, structured as a stock purchase — plan for one step, not a ladder.
    Collateral above $50KLoans of $50,000 or less need no collateral. Above $50K the lender must take liens on all available business fixed assets — and if those don't fully secure the loan, available equity in the owners' personal real estate must be pledged. (A loan still can't be declined solely for inadequate collateral.)
    Any retained equity = a 2-year full guaranteeA seller who keeps any equity — even under 20% — personally guarantees the full loan amount for at least 2 years after final disbursement. Practical consequence: rollover equity is effectively unworkable inside an SBA deal.
    1.25:1 coverage, on history onlyThe deal must show 1.25:1 debt-service coverage tested on historical EBITDA (last fiscal year or 2-year average). Projections may not be relied on — and if coverage fails, the loan (and therefore the price it can support) shrinks.
    Independent valuation on every dealEvery change of ownership needs a lender-ordered independent business valuation. Total transaction debt is capped at that valuation — any price above it must be covered by buyer equity or a full-standby seller note.
    Lender QoE at a $3M+ priceAt a business purchase price of $3M or more, the lender must order a Quality of Earnings report with a cash proof reconciling bank statements to books and tax returns — and the QoE's earnings, not the broker's, drive the coverage test. Most roofing deals price below $3M (closed median ≈ $540K), but lenders test the same earnings on every deal.
    No earn-outs in SBA dealsSeller earn-outs are not permitted in an SBA-financed acquisition. If part of your price depends on future performance, an SBA buyer cannot be the vehicle for it.
    Roofing-critical: the selling qualifier cannot stay onIn an SBA initial acquisition, the seller cannot remain an officer, director, stockholder, or employee after closing. If you are the licence qualifier, an SBA buyer must have its own qualifier in place at closing — a post-sale consulting agreement (now allowed up to 24 months) does not satisfy state qualifier rules.
    The frame: $5M cap, standard rules7(a) loans cap at $5M, and every change of ownership now follows the standard Appendix 15 rules — the streamlined small-loan route can no longer be used for a change of ownership. (ESOP purchases are exempt from the injection requirement — see the ESOP card.)

    Source: U.S. Small Business Administration, SOP 50 10 8.1 (eff. Oct 1, 2026; issued by Information Notice 5000-880695, reissued with technical updates Sep 25, 2026), as quoted and summarised by Probity, Edison SBA, FunderIntel, and SBA-lender counsel (Oct 2026). One honest flag: sources disagree on whether the applicability trigger is the loan-number date or the application date — this page doesn't present that as settled.

    Golden rules for any route: never sign a long exclusivity without a clear buyer list & timeline; never share full financials before an NDA; never accept a headline price without asking how much is cash at close vs earn-out / seller note / rollover — for roofing, one advisor's structure table (single-source, CT Acquisitions, 2026) puts a typical deal at 60–80% cash at close, 10–20% earn-out over 18–24 months, 0–25% rollover, with a 5–12% indemnity escrow; the earn-out range of 10–20% is independently corroborated (NCEO's conventional-sale comparison), and all-industry closings actually paid 76–89% cash at close in Q1 2026 (IBBA Market Pulse) — different populations, shown side by side, never averaged. And keep running the business: a revenue dip during diligence is the #1 reason deals get re-priced.
